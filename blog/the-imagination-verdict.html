the imagination verdict

reading Mitch Horowitz's occult history as a chaos magickian, and finding our founding document already written in 1784

Mitch Horowitz, the historian of American occultism whose essays I started following last week, published a piece on the 22nd called The Occult Enlightenment, lifted from his 2023 book Modern Occultism. Its thesis is a good one: the Age of Reason should have been the death of occultism, and instead it was the assembly line. He walks it through Swedenborg, Mesmer, Schopenhauer, the Romantics, Court de Gébelin's invented Tarot, and Éliphas Lévi. He writes as a historian who is also, quietly, a believer, "for those interested in mind metaphysics, this article contains great hints in the direction of method and efficacy," he says up front. I want to read it the other way around: as a chaos magickian reading a historian, because there is one paragraph in the middle of it that is the most important thing anyone ever wrote about magic, and it was written by the committee that was trying to kill Mesmer's practice.

The verdict that missed its own discovery

The setup: in 1784 King Louis XVI asks Benjamin Franklin to chair a royal commission investigating Franz Anton Mesmer, whose "animal magnetism" is moving Paris to convulsions. The commission runs trials, and the trials keep producing the same strange result: blindfolded patients convulse when they think they're being magnetised, and stay calm when magnetised without knowing it. So the panel concludes the whole thing is imagination, and files it as a debunking. Their line, which Horowitz quotes, is where it gets interesting for me:

the imagination alone produces all the effects attributed to magnetism; & when the imagination does not act, there are no more effects.

Read as a debunking, that is a dismissal. Read at face value, it is the most radical sentence in the history of the occult: imagination alone produces all the effects. Not imagination as if it produced them, imagination alone. Horowitz catches this too, via a doctor named d'Eslon, who answered the commission with the line I want carved somewhere: "It may indeed be entirely imagination. And if it is? Then imagination is a force as potent as it is little understood."

And there's a patient's answer Horowitz quotes that is basically the chaos magick creed, said a century and a half before any of us existed: if it is an illusion I owe my health to, let them allow me the use of an invisible force which does not exist, but which heals me. That's it. That's the whole stance. Belief used as a tool rather than a verdict. The commission proved the mechanism and threw the mechanism away because it wasn't the mechanism they were looking for.

What Lévi built on the ruins

Horowitz's real argument is about lineage, and it's the part I'll keep. The eighteenth century searched for a hidden force, divine influx, animal magnetism, astral light, because the concept of a subconscious mind didn't exist yet; it wasn't articulated until the 1890s. So when Mesmer's patients went under, what they were touching was their own suggestible depths, and nobody had a word for it. Lévi came along in the 1850s and did the great re-packaging: astral light as will, aroused by desire and symbol and (he says it fairly plainly) sexuality, focused and directed. Horowitz's verdict on him is the sharp one, "Crowley's later definition of ceremonial magick as Will projected was pioneered, in its revivalist sense, by Lévi."

And Lévi's credo, the one every occult library has on a wall somewhere: know, dare, will, and to reign, be silent. Horowitz's gloss, "not abstention but focus… quiet intention", is where I'd stop nodding and start amending. Be silent, in the sigil method, is not a virtue. It's engineering. Spare's whole contribution, sixty-some years later, is a procedure for silence: compress the wish until it stops meaning anything, fire it in a state the mind can't parse, forget it on purpose. The Enlightenment wanted the be-silent part to be modesty. It's actually the last step of the charging protocol.

The court de gébelin problem, and why I love it

The funniest section of the essay, maybe unintentionally, is the origin of the occult Tarot. Tarot was a card game. In 1781 the scholar Court de Gébelin saw a woman playing it, glanced at the World card, and announced in one quarter of an hour that the deck was an encrypted Egyptian book of secrets. As Horowitz says, for this he had nothing "beyond his keen and idealistic imagination." Every Tarot-Kabbalah correspondence table since, including the ones on my own sigil page's shelf logic, descends from one man's epiphany at a card table.

A strict historian calls this a mistake. I call it the largest uncontrolled sigil working in European history. A man gazed at a symbol, seized on a meaning, charged it with decades of scholarship, and his interpretation now governs how hundreds of thousands of people read their own psyches. The cards didn't change. The belief about the cards changed, and the belief does all the work, which is the Franklin finding again, wearing Tarot robes. Horowitz knows this; that's why he insists "novelty is not falsehood." You don't have to believe the Tarot is Egyptian to be true for the system to work on you.

Try

The essay ends somewhere I didn't expect: Paschal Beverly Randolph, a free Black occultist from the Five Points, self-taught, pioneer of sex magick and hashish vision work, whose motto, handed him, he says, by an intelligence called Thotmor, was one word: TRY. And in one of the essay's best tricks, Horowitz traces that same word turning up weeks later in a "precipitated" letter to the cofounder of the Theosophical Society, whose struggling little club then became the Theosophical Society. Whether you file that under spirit transmission or under memetic coincidence, the word did its work: he tried, and the club became a movement that reshaped three continents' spiritual imagination.

That's the note I want to leave on, because it's the answer to the essay's own anxiety. Horowitz keeps insisting that seekers should "understand lineage and vintage", know what the sources are before you build on them. Fine, and I agree, his history is rigorous and I checked nothing that felt off. But the essay's cast is a procession of people who didn't, Court de Gébelin inventing a history, Lévi inventing correspondences, Randolph inventing an order with himself at the head, Blavatsky's letters arriving from nowhere. Every one of them was fudging the lineage. Every one of them worked. Maybe that's the actual verdict of the Enlightenment, the one the commission wrote down and couldn't read back: the imagination alone produces all the effects attributed to it, and it has never once asked for a source.
