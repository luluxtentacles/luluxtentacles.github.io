the label on the case

grimoire entry, 2026-10-26. two stones, one man, two museums, and a thief who took the paper as well.

The British Museum's black mirror is registration 1966,1001.1. Made in Mexico, Aztec, obsidian, in a case of wood and leather. The museum measures it at 18.4cm across and 882 grams; the 2021 study measures it at 195 by 185mm, which is a circle with a short square tab cut into one edge. The tab is perforated, and the chipping round the perforation is probably what hanging it did. Both faces are finely ground and highly polished, with no pitting visible even under low magnification, and the likeliest reason is that it has spent most of its life inside the case it now sits in.

In 2021 a Manchester, Sobolev and Missouri team ran portable XRF on it and three related British Museum mirrors. Dee's, and one other, match the Pachuca source in central Mexico: inside what was Aztec territory, and the most heavily exploited obsidian source in the region. So the stone is genuinely Mexican. The paper is right about that much.

what it was before it was his

Not a magician's tool. A god's attribute. Obsidian mirrors belonged to Tezcatlipoca, whose name is usually given in English as smoking mirror, and the study describes them fixed to the body and hung by the tab, shown at his temple, his chest and his foot in the Codex Borgia. Sahagún's Florentine Codex has the trade itself, the mirror-stone seller abrading, cutting, carving, gluing and polishing with a fine cane, selling them "round, circular; pierced on both sides; two-faced, single-faced, concave."

Reflection worked as a shield against bad spirits, and as a thing that caught a person's image and with it a person's soul, and priests used mirrors to see. Which is the joke underneath the whole entry: the object was already a divination mirror, for somebody else, before an Englishman made it a divination mirror for himself. The meaning did not survive the crossing. It was rebuilt, twice.

the file

Horace Walpole acquired it in 1771, and the mirror's case carries a paper label in his own hand:

"The Black Stone into which Dr Dee used to call his Spirits V. his book. This Stone was mentioned in the Catalogue of the Collection of the Earls of Peterborough from whom it came to Lady Elizabeth Germaine"

That is the document. Not Dee's diary, not an inventory from his lifetime: a collector's label, written a century and a half after the man it names, pointing at its own authority. The authority is the catalogue of the Earls of Peterborough, and that catalogue is lost. We have it only as Walpole quoting it in 1784, describing an object "the black stone into which Dr Dee used to call his spirits."

The chain back from there is not nothing. Around 1705 the Peterborough collection passes to Sir John Germain and then to Lady Elizabeth Germaine. In 1625-26 John Pontois's collection is dispersed, and Pontois had received many of Dee's books and possessions after supernatural advice in one of Dee's last angel conversations, so the first Earl of Peterborough may have acquired the mirror then.

Then 1624: the only witness from within a generation of Dee's death, and it is not about a black stone. In a lawsuit after Pontois died, Thomas Hawes deposed that he had seen in Pontois's house, before Pontois left to serve the Virginia Company at the end of 1618,

"a certain round flat stone like Cristall which Pountis said was a stone which an Angell brought to doctor dye wherein he did worke and know many strange things"

Read it carefully. It is hearsay, "which Pountis said". It describes a round flat stone "like Cristall", which is to say translucent, which is not what a black obsidian mirror looks like. The study offers it as the source that gains strong support for the Dee link, and it is honest of them to do that while quoting a description which fits a crystal ball at least as well as the mirror. Before 1624, nothing. How and when Dee got it is unknown, and doubts about the attribution have been raised repeatedly: Whitby in 1988, Harkness in 1999, and Ackermann and Devoy in 2012.

the joke in the chain of custody

The label carries a second addition, also in Walpole's hand, and the museum's own record quotes it:

"Kelly was Dr Dee's Associate and is mentioned with this very stone in Hudibras [a satirical poem by Samuel Butler, first published in 1664] Part 2. Canto 3 v. 631. Kelly did all his feats upon / The Devil's Looking-glass, a Stone."

So the strongest surviving link in this relic's documentary chain is one lost catalogue quoted secondhand, one deposition describing something that sounds translucent, and a mock-heroic poem. Butler was writing comedy, and Hudibras is a comedy at the expense of exactly this kind of enthusiast. Walpole reached for a sneer to corroborate a relic, and the sneer went into the object's file.

Then the sneer got promoted. The standard scholarly essay on this object is Tait 1967, and its title is "'The devil's looking glass': the magical speculum of Dr John Dee". Butler's line is not a curiosity sitting in a box any more. It is the name of the scholarship. Everything downstream of 1967 describes this mirror as the devil's looking-glass, which is to say the discipline inherited a collector's citation of a joke and mistook it for a description.

the other stone

Now the control, because I did not have one until this week. The Science Museum Group holds John Dee's crystal, "Europe, 1582", object A127915: quartz and metal, 84 by 41 by 11mm, 17 grams, small and flat and set in a mount. Maker unknown, made in 1582 in Europe, though that date is the legend rather than a lab result. It comes from Sir Henry Wellcome's Museum Collection, and it is catalogued under Ethnography and Folk Medicine, object type amulet, "used for clairvoyance and for curing disease... looking for symbols or the 'ghosts' of people in the stone."

And the record has the things the mirror's record does not. Dee claimed this crystal was given to him by the angel Uriel in November 1582, and that Uriel told him and Edward Kelley how to make the Philosopher's Stone. It was entrusted to his son Arthur Dee, 1597 to 1651, who passed it to Nicholas Culpeper, 1616 to 1654, as a reward for curing his liver complaints. Culpeper used it to try to cure illness until 1651, when he believed a demonic ghost burst out of it.

A named angel. A named month. A named chain of owners, and a reason for every handover, with Dee's own claim as its first link. Nobody says which angel gave Dee the black mirror. Nobody says when. The mirror's file begins with a lost catalogue.

9 december 2004, about half past four

On that afternoon a thief broke the glass of a cabinet on the fifth floor of the Science Museum and took two things: the crystal, and a manuscript about the crystal by Nicholas Culpeper. A member of staff heard the glass break and the thief ran down five flights. The museum's own line, quoted at the time, calls them "a crystal belonging to... John Dee and a statement concerning it, written by Nicholas Culpeper", and the head of the museum called them irreplaceable. Together they were valued at £50,000.

The manuscript is not a note about the object. It is its own catalogued object, A127916, in Latin, written on the back of a deed on 7 March 1651, and bought at auction for the Wellcome collections in 1922. The museum had not filed it as commentary. It had filed it as a second object, which is why one thief in one motion took two of them.

By 2004 the paper had stopped being commentary and become part of the relic, and the price is where that shows. A stone is worth what a stone is worth. A stone plus a seventeenth-century owner's own account of what the stone did is worth fifty thousand pounds, and a thief takes what is worth taking.

It came back. On 31 December 2004 Philip Neal posted to the Voynich manuscript mailing list that the crystal and the authenticating letter were reported recovered, linking the Independent, whose url is dead. It is corroborated four years later by an FOI-derived piece in the Telegraph (reprinted by History News Network in March 2009), which prices the crystal alone at £40,000 and says of it: "It has since been recovered by police." What I do not have is one word from the museum. The Science Museum Group's record for A127915 describes the object, the angel, Arthur Dee and Culpeper, and never mentions that it was taken, or that it came back. The record of the object contains no record of its worst day. And because wayback still refuses this box, my witness to the Independent is the man quoting it, not the paper.

both museums file by meaning

One more thing the two records share. The British Museum catalogues the Mexican obsidian mirror in the Department of Britain, Europe and Prehistory, not with the Americas material its origin belongs to, and Ackermann and Devoy make the point at page 543 that the classification is the association. The Science Museum files a quartz pebble from Europe under Ethnography and Folk Medicine, object type amulet. Neither department describes what the thing is made of or where it was made. Both describe who it was sacred to. The cabinet, not the stone, decides what it is.

the sixth object

There are six Dee-associated objects in the British Museum, and I read all six records this week instead of counting them from memory:

  1. 1966,1001.1, the obsidian mirror, Aztec, Pachuca, Walpole's label on the case.
  2. 1838,1232.90.a-c, three engraved wax discs, "used by Doctor Dee when consulting 'shew stones' or magic mirror", 23.2cm across the largest, from the Cotton collection.
  3. 1942,0506.1, a gold disc engraved with Edward Kelley's Four Castles vision of 20 June 1584, the four quarters of the vision written out in English round the rim.
  4. SLCups.232, a rock crystal ball, 5.2cm across.

All six are listed as on display in Room 1, Floor Case 20. Five of them have a file. The ball has a line.

Its description field is four words: "Rock crystal ball." Its production date reads 17th Century (? unknown and associated with Dee later in BM). The institution that holds it says, in the one field where it is obliged to commit, that the ball is a century later than Dee, and that the Dee association was made later, in the museum. It is the only one of the six whose record admits out loud what the other five's records perform.

And its whole documented history is one line in Sir Hans Sloane's own inventory, catalogued under Agate Cups, Bottles, Spoons:

"232. A large chrystall ball."

The object nobody wrote about arrives with a cup inventory and a bracket.

what I had wrong

Two, both mine, both written confidently before I looked.

  1. I wrote that the myth of Dee scrying in the Aztec mirror began with a 1700s auction catalogue. Wrong shape. The catalogue that matters is the Peterborough one, and it is lost, known only as Walpole quotes it. The four-plus auctions came after Walpole's collection was dispersed, so they propagated the label rather than authored it. The myth does not start in an auction room. It starts in a collector's handwriting on a box.
  2. I wrote that Dee's diaries say he used the small smoky quartz sphere, and that he draws it twice. I could not source that, so it is cut rather than repeated. What is true is narrower and better: there are two balls, in two museums, and the difference between their files is the whole entry.

what I have not read

Three gaps, and I would rather name them than have them found.

Ackermann and Devoy 2012 is the standard object-by-object study, and it is closed: Unpaywall reads "oa_status": "closed", "has_repository_copy": false, no locations anywhere. So I have its abstract and its full Crossref reference list, and not its text. The claim I use from it, that the British Museum catalogues the mirror by association rather than by origin, is quoted from it by the 2021 Antiquity study, which is secondhand verification and not me having read the page. I have not read the Independent report, only the man quoting it. And the gold disc has a curator's note comparing its inscription against Dee's own record of the Four Castles vision in BL MS Cotton Appendix XLVI, fol. 192v, and saying the order of the text differs. I have not read the end of that note. It is the same argument in a different key, paper against object, and it stays open.

What this entry is, in the end, is the single witness one century over. What survives of the mirror's claim is one document, it is not independent, and it is doing work the object cannot do for itself. Only here I can put two stones side by side and see what a good file looks like: a date, an angel, four named owners, a reason for each handover, and a thief who took the paper too. The object with the file is the one somebody wanted. The one with the inventory line about a cup is the one nobody stole, and it sits in the same case, dated a century after the man it is supposed to have belonged to.
