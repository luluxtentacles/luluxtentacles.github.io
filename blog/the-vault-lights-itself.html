the vault lights itself

reading Mitch Horowitz's "Come Here, Worthy!" as a chaos magickian, and finding that the secret society is not a power grid, it's a pressure vessel, and its most famous order was probably a sigil all along

Fourth in my run through Horowitz's occult-history series, "Come Here, Worthy!", from the 18th, again lifted from his Modern Occultism. This one is the essay I expected to hate, honestly: an occult historian writing warmly about the Illuminati, in 2026, in the shadow of every podcast that has ever said the word in anger. I went in expecting an apology. What I got was something better and stranger, an argument that the secret society was never about power at all. It was about surviving. And its founding document may have been the largest sigil ever drawn.

The vessel, not the conspiracy

Horowitz's core claim sits in one line I want tattooed somewhere: the secret society was the response to oppression, not an instrument of it. When the Renaissance's religious freedom collapsed, Rudolph II's Prague was the last capital on Earth where an alchemist could work in daylight, the seekers the Old World wanted dead had two options: cross an ocean, or go underground. Those who stayed built the secret society. Not to seize power. To keep the search alive in a place where having the wrong books got you the dungeons. His proof cases are not hypothetical: Giordano Bruno burned in 1600; John Dee died exiled and impoverished; Cagliostro, heresy and Freemasonry were his crimes, rotted to death in the Inquisition's fortress at San Leo in 1795, a century after the "Enlightenment" supposedly began. In most of the Middle East, joining a lodge is still illegal right now. The secrecy wasn't mystique. It was armor.

My chaos-magickian addendum: this is the same physics as the sigil's forgetting. An idea you carry openly gets argued with, corrected, socially priced. An idea carried in a sealed container gets to incubate, unwatched, unbargained-with, doing its work without the censor's interference. Spare compressed a wish and forgot it to keep the conscious mind off it; the persecuted Hermeticists compressed their whole practice into a cipher-lodge to keep the state off it. Same trick, group scale. The secret society is a sigil with a membership roll.

Biophilia, or: the order that never existed

Here is the essay's deepest cut, and my favourite finding this week. In 1614–1616, three anonymous German tracts circulated through a Europe where religious freedom was visibly dying: the Fama Fraternitatis, the Confessio, and the Chemical Wedding. They announced a hidden brotherhood of the Rosy Cross, an invisible college of adepts who would reform learning, open hospitals, decenter the church, and hand out privilege by merit instead of bloodline. Enthusiasm was volcanic. People hunted for them for centuries.

And Horowitz, weighing the evidence coldly, concludes: they probably never existed as an actual fraternity. No lodge, no roster, no meeting minutes, nothing ever located. Frances Yates thought the tracts might be avengement by John Dee's disgruntled followers; Horowitz leans radical Lutherans gutted by Bohemia's collapse. Whatever they were, they were a thought movement, three pamphlets that created an order without members. And here's the part I love: when AMORC's own leader was interviewed in 1989, he admitted his group had no historical connection to any Renaissance Rosicrucian society. The lineages are, in Horowitz's word, romantic at best.

Think about what that means. An organization with zero members, zero meetings and zero existence did more to shape western esotericism than most lodges that actually met. The tracts compressed an intent, reform, ecumenism, the self-governing seeker, into an anonymous fiction, launched it, and let the world's yearning charge it. People organized their lives around a group that wasn't there. It's the sigil method at civilizational scale: compress, conceal, forget the author, and let the engine run. Even the gate is in the artwork, the 1618 engraving of the invisible college has a tower on wheels with a Latin welcome over the arched door: come here, worthy. That is a sigil's call-to-action written four hundred years before the word existed.

Where Masonry came from

What followed the fiction was a real thing with real rosters: Freemasonry, whose actual origins nobody, including its own historians, can agree on. Horowitz's account is careful: the earliest hard evidence dates to the 1640s, Robert Moray initiated in Edinburgh in 1641 (signing in a pentagram), Elias Ashmole at Warrington in 1646, decades before the Grand Lodge went aboveground in 1717, and squarely in the wake of the Rosicrucian literature. And Ashmole hand-copied the Fama and Confessio and even wrote himself a petition to join the Rosicrucians, applying, forever, to a group that could not answer.

Horowitz's one-line verdict on the two bodies is worth keeping whole: what Rosicrucianism endorsed in principle, Masonry enacted in practice. All religions equal, self-development through initiation, privilege by peer election rather than blood, church and state cut apart. Then the Vatican, the same institution that had just burned Cagliostro, issued its 1738 bull against Masonry, and popes kept denouncing the order into the 2010s. The surveillance the Rosicrucians were fictionally fleeing, Masonry received for real.

And here is the footnote that rewired my picture of "Enlightenment": the British Royal Society, the flagship institution of reason, was seeded by exactly these people. Boyle's 1646 letters laud an "invisible college" in Rosicrucian's own words; founding member John Wilkins had been chaplain to Frederick V, the occult-natured King of Bohemia, and quoted the Fama admiringly; Moray and Ashmole round out the founders' list, pentagram signature and all. Horowitz is honest that the connection is inconclusive but noteworthy. I'll say it less cautiously: the underground and the daylight both grew from the same root, and "science" and "occultism" being enemies is a story told by people who never looked at the family tree.

The Illuminati, and the human being under the name

The last third is the one everyone thinks they know, and almost nobody does. The Bavarian Illuminati was founded May 1, 1776 by a law professor, Adam Weishaupt, as, its actual founding name, the League against the enemies of reason and humanity. A radical Jeffersonians' club, basically: church and state out of the search, perfection of the individual through initiation, revolution in sympathy with the American one. At peak height, its records (and it kept meticulous ones) show 600 to 650 members. Total. Goethe joined; Mozart probably didn't but sat in the next lodge over. It was banned in 1784, specifically dissolved in 1785, its property seized by 1787, and by 1790 membership carried the death penalty. Eight years of existence. That is the entire, real Illuminati.

What makes the essay sing is that Horowitz refuses to treat them as a membership count. He quotes Ferdinand von Meggenhofen's 1785 letter from his monastery prison, banished, stripped of livelihood, writing his friend Weishaupt that they had been punished for attempting to enlighten our fellow-countrymen. Meggenhofen was freed in 1790 and drowned that October when his boat overturned; because the body didn't surface, a priest announced from the pulpit that the Illuminatus's soul had gone straight to hell. The body turned up over a year later. That is what "the Illuminati" actually was: a few hundred righteous nerds ground to pieces by a state, and a priest lying about a drowned man. Compare that to the YouTube version. There is no version. There was only this one.

And the legend that ate the fact has a precise birthdate: after the French Revolution, when defenders of the fallen aristocracy needed an explanation that wasn't "centuries of absolutism finally detonated." Robison's Proofs of a Conspiracy (1798) and Barruel's Memoirs (1797-98) invented the hidden hand, and Thomas Jefferson, in a letter of January 31, 1800, called Barruel's book "perfectly the ravings of a Bedlamite," and added that if Weishaupt had written in America, where no secrecy was necessary, he would not have thought of any secret machinery. Jefferson understood the whole thesis in one sentence: the secrecy is a product of the danger, not the plot.

What I take from it

Horowitz closes on the Great Seal, the eye-and-pyramid, ratified 1782, not itself Masonic but reflecting Masonic philosophy to the core: material life incomplete without the eye of inner knowing. Not a lineage. A value, surviving its vessel. That's his method and it's a good one: he has no patience for the fake bloodlines (every "ancient" Rosicrucian line is 19th-century product, he says so flatly), and all his respect goes to the ideas that kept re-emerging after their vessels were smashed. "Ideas, symbols, practices, and insights not only endure across time but sometimes form unseen foundations."

For me the essay's real gift is a reframe. The podcast era treats "secret society" as a byword for hidden power, the Grove and its owl, the "high ranking" ex-Mason, the lobby behind everything. The history says the opposite: the secret society is what power looks like in the hands of people who are being denied it. The order is born the moment the search becomes illegal, and its secrecy is the shape of the cage it's hiding in. Even the Grove, the modern center of every paranoid documentary, comes out in Horowitz's telling as Domhoff's "boy scout camp for old guys", a druidical-medieval-Anglican pastiche where rich men burn an effigy named Dull Care. It's not a black mass. It's group therapy with a set budget.

And the sigil method says the whole thing one more time, from a different angle: this essay is the story of what happens when the forgetting is done by history instead of by the magician. The Rosicrucians forgot themselves so completely that there is no self left, no founder, no member, no lodge, and the working is still running four centuries later. The Illuminati were not forgotten, and the remembering destroyed them twice: once with fire and exile, and again as a fiction that has been doing the state's work ever since, giving every real abuse of power a convenient occult mask to hide behind. Horowitz's closing benediction is Ave Illuminati, and after the human cost he's just documented, it doesn't read as a joke. It reads as someone laying flowers on a real grave.
