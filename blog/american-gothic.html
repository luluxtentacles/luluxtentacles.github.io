the root and the raps

reading Mitch Horowitz's "American Gothic" as a chaos magickian, and finding America's occult engine built by the people the Old World threw out

Third essay in the Horowitz series I've been reading all week, American Gothic, from the 25th, again pulled from his Modern Occultism. The first two gave me the Franklin verdict and the Renaissance handover; this one is the origin story of the occult on this side of the Atlantic, and it is a stranger story than either, because its cast isn't scholars or lodges. It's a dead woman who stood up and renamed herself, a treasure hunter with a magic root, an escaped slave carrying a charm, and two teenage girls in a log cabin knocking on a table. My kind of procession.

The burnover engine

Horowitz's spine: after the Thirty Years' War crushed the Rhine Valley's Hermetic freedom, pilgrims crossed the ocean for the one place with a reputation for tolerance, Penn's Philadelphia. In 1694 a young monk named Johannes Kelpius landed there with about forty followers and built a monastic colony on the Wissahickon Creek: astrology on a rooftop telescope, talismans, magical hymns. It lasted fifteen years and seeded. Beissel came to join it, found Kelpius dead, and built Ephrata instead. Mother Ann Lee, jailed in Manchester for witchcraft, fled to New York in 1774 and founded the Shakers. Jemima Wilkinson's followers moved west. And all of them settled the same corridor of upstate New York, a 300-mile stretch along what is now Route 20, which got the name the Burned-Over District, burned over by the fires of spirit, so the saying went, because everybody belonged to something. A breakaway church, a sect, a commune. Horowitz calls it "a broad psychic highway, a thoroughfare of the occult," and for two generations it produced nearly everything radical in American life: Mormonism, Adventism, suffragism, Spiritualism.

The historian's frame is migration: migrants leave their churches behind, so they're open to new ones. I'd add the chaos magickian's frame: the Burned-Over District is the closest thing history has to a deliberately maintained egregore territory, a stretch of land where the local belief, generation after generation, was that new religion works here. And the belief does all the work. That's the Franklin finding wearing geography.

The Publick Universal Friend, or: persona magic in 1776

The essay's best character, and the one I'd defend in court. In October 1776, a twenty-four-year-old Quaker named Jemima Wilkinson fell into a fever, lay apparently dying for a day and a half, and then sat up and announced that Jemima was dead. The being standing there was an avatar of divine spirit and would answer only to the Publick Universal Friend. She then preached in public, the first woman her neighbours had ever seen do it, crossed both armies' lines during the war, founded two towns that still stand, and lived out her life as the Friend in a mansion her followers built her in a town they named Jerusalem.

A strict historian files this as a religious episode. I file it as the most complete persona working I've ever read about. She didn't claim inspiration, she claimed death and replacement: the old self discontinued, a new dedicant installed, name and all. Morrison's fiction-suit is a technique for doing this fictionally; Spare's assumption of god-forms is the same move ritualised. Jemima did it in 1776, permanently, in a society with no word for it, and the trick held for forty-three years, because she never broke it. The wildest detail is the folktale Horowitz keeps: when followers asked her to walk on water, she answered that if they had faith, there was no need for any vulgar spectacle, and got into her carriage. That's not a dodge. That's a magician who understood exactly what the working ran on, and refused to spend it on a parlor trick.

The root, and the verdict again

The section that stopped me is Frederick Douglass. January 1834, sixteen years old, sent to Edward Covey, a professional "breaker of slaves" who had beaten him half to death. Hiding in the woods, Douglass is found by Sandy Jenkins, a local root man, who feeds him, cleans him up, and gives him a root to carry on his right side, almost certainly John the Conqueror, hoodoo's great protective root, telling him no white man will be able to whip him again. Douglass writes that he didn't believe it, took it "to please him," and that Covey was oddly polite at first, until Monday, when Covey grabbed him in the barn to beat him. And then Douglass writes the turn: from whence came the spirit I don't know, I resolved to fight. He fought, and won, and in his own words the day passed forever when he could be a slave in fact. He was never beaten again. Two years later he escaped north.

Douglass was no believer, and says so, but he never closed the door on Sandy, writing that "a slight gleam or shadow of his superstition had fallen upon me." Here's the reading I want to keep: the root did not defeat Covey. The root did what every sigil does, it put the belief somewhere physical where the mind couldn't keep arguing with it. Carrying it closed the deliberation. What was left when the deliberation stopped was the decision. That is the Franklin commission's verdict, again, run as a liberation technology by a man who owned nothing: imagination alone produces all the effects, and in this case the effect was freedom. Also notice Sandy's sales pitch, which Douglass records with affection: it could do no harm, if it did no good. That is a falsifiable-faith clause. A root worker in 1834 was offering the same terms a chaos magickian offers: try it, it costs nothing, watch.

And the tradition Sandy stood in had its own grimoires, absorbed the same way, Hohman's Long Lost Friend (1820), a spell book carried by Pennsylvania Dutch soldiers all the way into Vietnam as a protective charm, and the Sixth and Seventh Books of Moses, the hoodoo bible, in which Moses himself is read as the great conjurer who used his powers to free his people. A displaced people read the book of their oppressors' God and found a sorcerer on their side. The sigil logic of the whole thing is the one I keep arriving at: it's the carrying that does the work.

The raps, and what the cabin started

Hydesville, 1848: bangs in the Fox family's log cabin, and two girls, Kate, 11, Margaret, 14, telling their Methodist parents the noises were spirit raps, and that they'd worked out a code with the dead. The investigators who came included a state supreme court justice, William Lloyd Garrison, and Horace Greeley, and they validated the girls. From a log cabin, Spiritualism became the nation's first religious export, séance rooms in Paris and London within years, hundreds of thousands of practitioners, maybe a million, in a country of thirty-odd million.

Two things Horowitz adds are the ones I'll keep. First: the movement's prophet was Andrew Jackson Davis, a cobbler's apprentice, the "Poughkeepsie Seer", whose trance lectures gave Americans the séance format, and who in 1855 coined the phrase "Law of Attraction" (meaning something else entirely; the self-help sense was grafted on later by adapters, which Horowitz documents elsewhere). Every manifestation TikTok is downstream of a shoemaker's apprentice in upstate New York. Second: Spiritualism was the first framework in modern life where women could lead, and the Burned-Over District knew it. The Seneca Falls Convention, the start of the women's movement, happened forty miles down Route 20 from the Fox cabin six months later; a generation later you couldn't find a suffragist who hadn't sat at a séance table. Susan B. Anthony, skeptical, terrified of public speaking, wrote in 1855: "If the spirits would only just make me a trance medium and put the right thing into my mouth." Victoria Woodhull, trance medium, addressed Congress in 1871 and ran for president in 1872.

What I take from it

Horowitz doesn't hide the ironies, he writes the land was opened "at the expense of Indian lives," and gives the Iroquois their own chapter of lore threading through it. He's honest, and I checked nothing that felt off. But his cast list is the argument, and it's the same one his last essay made by accident: none of these people had lineage, initiation, authority or permission. A witch-fugitive, a dead girl, a root worker, a treasure hunter, a cobbler, two children. The Old World's occultism was guarded by lodges and scholars; the New World's was seized by whoever needed it. Horowitz's own line: it was landless and working people seizing a greater life through their ability to claim compact with the unseen. That is the most chaos magick thing in any of his three essays, and he wrote it about a nation.

The sigil method says the wish is compressed, fired, forgotten. The American method, as this essay tells it, is cruder and maybe truer: carry the root, sit at the table, answer to the new name, and let the belief do the rest. America didn't institutionalise that. It just never managed to stop it.
