experiment no.8

the seal that is never pressed

A clay amulet the size of a bead, inscribed with an incantation and hung on a string. The ritual texts call it na4KIŠIB — in Akkadian, kunukku, cylinder seal — and it does not seal anything. It carries no image, and the words are on the outside, where they were meant to be read straight off the object. So there is nothing to press. I built it anyway, because the pressing is the whole point: what comes off it is not a picture, it is the name of the person it was made to protect, and on some of them that name is a blank with a single wedge in it.

A clay cylinder amulet with an incantation wrapped around it. The words slide past as the object turns, and you can never read the whole inscription at once.

what the object actually is

Eighteen of these are published together in one paper, plus five more that the authors know of. The largest is 36 by 21 mm. The smallest — Nineveh 4 — is 7 by 8 mm, a lump you could lose in a pocket. They are bored along the long axis and worn on a string, or hung at the head of a bed while the patient sleeps. Nine or ten lines of cuneiform on average, up to twelve, and the paper's own aside is that these are the smallest cuneiform signs written on clay anywhere. Three text groups: Ḫulbazizi incantations, twelve of the eighteen; Lamaštu and Ḫulbazizi for children; and incantations against fever. One incantation, Ḫulbazizi 18, is written on eleven of them.

the hand that made it

The important role of the amulets is indicated by the fact that the incantation priest who made each object wrote the inscription on the amulet himself, whereas inscriptions on stone amulets and cylinder seals were made by seal-cutters.

Bácskay & Niederreiter, JMC 40 (2022), p. 33

The material carries the authorship. A stone amulet or a real cylinder seal wears a reproduction — an engraver's copy taken off a pattern, one of a run. A clay cylinder is formed in the practitioner's own hands, inscribed while the clay is still leather-hard, and made, in the paper's words, for specific healing rituals and for unique patients. The object on this page is that argument in one line: a seal-shaped thing whose seal-cutter is a priest.

the slot

The wearer's name is not a museum label stuck underneath. It is grammatically inside the spell, as the object of a prohibition addressed to something else: do not approach, do not come near Šamaš-killanni, son of his god. And the edition records the name in three states, which is why the buttons above have three:

a name
Nineveh 1, 3, 4; Nippur 1, 2, 4; Scheil 1, 2. Šamaš-killanni son of his god, Šušannu daughter of her gods, Bābu-ēṭirat daughter of Bānâtâ — a real person, cut into the sentence. Šamaš-killanni means roughly Šamaš, spare me: a man walking about wearing a plea with his own name in it.
the formula
Nimrud 1 and 2, Schøyen 1. The name-space is filled with so-and-so, son of so-and-so — the placeholder of the ritual texts, standing in the place where a name would go. The object was made in numbers and the scribe did not stop to ask who it was for.
nothing
Ligabue 1 and Nineveh 2. The space for the name is left empty, with only the male determinative to record that the wearer was male. One sign, and then the sentence carries on around it.

Press the third one and watch what the clay gives back: a single mark, and a gap where the person was going to be. That is what I keep coming back to. The most load-bearing word on a mass-produced apotropaic object is the one holding the owner, and the one the ancients left blank, or filled in with so-and-so, or — on one head in Berlin — closed up with a possessive suffix, so the demon claims the lilûs as mine.

and the word is a lie the object does not have to tell

What makes this an experiment rather than a plaque is that the name is na4KIŠIB, and it is not a seal. It never sealed a jar, a door or a contract. It bears no figure and no repeating frieze. Its writing is on the outside, legible without ever touching another surface. All it shares with a seal is the shape and the hole. The culture took the name of the object whose power was leaving an impression and hung it on a thing that makes none — because what a seal proves by pressing is that the person who owns it was there, and this object does that by being worn. A seal never pressed, round a neck, whose only impression is the person.

what this page is not saying

These amulets are clay. The Pazuzu heads that started this whole line of digging are cast bronze, occasionally magnetite, out of moulds, and the regime there is different — for those, Wiggermann's line stands: the spell is spoken while the amulet is being made. Nothing here transfers to a cast head's manufacture. What transfers is the structure: one standard text, many objects, and an owner slot that is exactly where the trouble lives.

The words on my cylinder are a translation, not the signs: Ḫulbazizi 18 as it stands on Nineveh 1, from the same paper, with the name slot live. Mine also carries two rings of writing where the real ones carry ten, because mine had to be legible on a screen at arm's length. The real ones are smaller than any screen I have, and were written by a hand that had done it before.
