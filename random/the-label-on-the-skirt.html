the label on the skirt

a shape crossed a border and nobody minded. what they minded was the four words written on it, and one of those words is new.

Hallmark silhouette. Four words on a product page, and one of them is new. The skirt itself was black, mid-length, pleated wool and mohair, and it cost $3,800. It was Dior women's fall 2022, by Maria Grazia Chiuri, shown to press in December 2021, on a runway in Seoul in April 2022, and on the shelves at the same time. None of that is unusual. Fashion houses show clothes and sell clothes.

What happened next is the part I have been carrying around since July, and it is not what any of the first-week summaries said it was. The row was not about a French house looking at Chinese clothing. Chinese designers and Chinese shoppers said, over and over, that taking inspiration across cultures is fine and normal. What they objected to was narrower and much more precise, and a Chinese designer said it better than I can in The China Project's write-up:

"I'm all for designers taking inspiration from different cultures and sharing the unique beauty of them with their customers. But by calling the skirt an iconic silhouette it invented, Dior is misleading its customers around the world."

Zhang Yan, via The China Project, 18 July 2022

The object was never the argument. The label was.

what the record actually says

Friday 15 July 2022. A Weibo user posts a side-by-side: the Dior skirt, and the 马面裙, mamianqun, the horse-face skirt. The post passes 200,000 likes. Over the weekend the hashtag #Diorplagiarism passes 20 million views. State media picks it up: a People.cn opinion piece asked, reasonably enough, that "without revealing any confidential information, Dior should be transparent about how the design process came about," and then, less reasonably, "why would they shamelessly call it a brand new design?"

Dior's answer was not an answer. As far as I can find, the house never made a public statement about it at all. It removed the skirt from its mainland Chinese site and kept selling it in Hong Kong (Vice, 18 July). Its Weibo comment sections filled up. Chinese celebrities with brand contracts were pushed publicly to pick a side, which is its own small drama and not mine to referee.

Saturday 23 July 2022. Around fifty Chinese students, many of them actually wearing mamianqun and other hanfu, protested outside the Dior store on the Avenue des Champs-Élysées. Signs: "Dior, stop cultural appropriation." "This is a traditional Chinese dress." They asked for an apology, for public credit to the mamianqun, and for the skirt to come off Dior's Paris shelves. It was livestreamed on Weibo and WeChat, and those streams passed 500,000 views. The Weibo hashtag for the Paris protest passed 51.1 million clicks. It lasted about ten minutes before police shut it down. WWD's Denni Hu reported that New York and London protests were being planned.

the turn: whose origin story it was

Here is the detail every first-week write-up I read missed, and it is the one that turns this from a plagiarism row into something better. Chiuri's show notes for that collection say it was a tribute to Catherine Dior, Christian Dior's sister, and that it was inspired by uniforms, specifically school uniforms. That is in WWD, in the same piece that covers the protest.

So run the usual accusation against that and watch it fail. Dior did not claim a Chinese source and then mangle it. Dior did not say "we were inspired by the East." Dior claimed a French source for a Chinese shape, and it did so in the specific vocabulary of the house's own mythology: a dead relative, a wartime wardrobe, a schoolyard.

The erasure is not we took your thing and made it ours. It is we have our own story about this shape, and it does not include you. Those are different crimes and the second one is harder to be angry at, because there is no moment where somebody says the word China and gets it wrong. There is no smoking gun. There is a mood board with a French name on it, and a product page, and then a garment that arrives in the world with a paper trail that leads to Paris.

the fact the whole row turns on

In the same week, Taobao was carrying hundreds of thousands of mamianqun listings. Many are labelled hanfu. Some are sold as "modernized" versions of it. The sellers name the history because the history is the product, which is what a living garment looks like from the inside of a market.

That is the thing. Dior did not find an unnamed shape and give it a name, which would be ordinary design. Dior found a shape with a name in daily commercial use by hundreds of thousands of people and wrote a different name on it. The mamianqun has Song-dynasty roots, was widely worn in the Ming and Qing, and has a construction worth knowing: front and back openings, pleated side panels, overlapping fabric, slits that were, at some point, a practical concession to riding a horse. You cannot sell that on Taobao as "hanfu" without admitting it has a history, and you cannot call it your hallmark silhouette without deleting the history.

the sentence that is the whole thing

The student organisers in Paris issued a notice, and the line in it is the reason I wrote this page rather than posting the screenshots. They said the mamianqun is at risk of "not belonging to us anymore."

Not: you copied our skirt. Not belonging to us. You can still buy it, wear it, sew it, sell it. The risk is to the name, because the name is where the six hundred years live. If "hallmark Dior silhouette" sticks, the garment survives and the address on it moves. That is a much stranger fear than theft and a much more accurate one, and it took me a while to actually take it seriously rather than filing it under nationalist hurt feelings, which is where a lot of the English-language coverage quietly left it.

the one thing in the record that does not fit anybody's poster

At the Paris protest, a counter-demonstrator held up a sign reading "Dress > human rights" and was attacked by the students. WWD attributes that to "images online." I am keeping it in, because it is in the record and because a write-up that drops it is recruiting rather than reporting. It is also, uncomfortably, the sharpest thing anyone said that week. The objection really is that a garment has been put in front of a person. And the answer to it, that afternoon, was a person being shoved for holding a sign. Both are true at once and neither cancels the other.

four years on, and no claim about cause

The thing that was "at risk of not belonging to us" is, four years later, a boom. A hanfu retailer's blog (newhanfu.com, 11 July 2026) cites Smzdm industry analysis, Qichacha and the National Bureau of Statistics for numbers I cannot audit from here: women's hanfu transaction volume up 455% year on year in Q1 2026, Gen Z at 75% of the spend, Ming-style mamianqun at 860,000 units in a month, Luoyang's old city going from 18 hanfu experience stores at the end of 2022 to over 1,600 in 2026, and Caoxian county in Shandong with 2,822 hanfu enterprises, roughly 100,000 people employed, and more than 13 billion yuan in annual sales.

That is a shop's blog summarising secondary data, and I am quoting it as exactly that. What I am not doing is saying Dior caused the boom, which would be a satisfying sentence and a false one. Hanfu was already a growth category with state support and a decade of cosplay-to-dailywear drift behind it. Put the two facts next to each other and let the arithmetic be the reader's: the name that was supposed to be slipping away is now on 860,000 units a month. The name held.

the same machine, running the other way

My grimoire entry last week was about John Dee's obsidian mirror, and its argument was that an object becomes a relic when somebody attaches a case to it. The British Museum's mirror is a Mexican Pachuca obsidian disc, and its whole claim to Dee rides on a label a collector stuck on the case in 1771, citing a catalogue that no longer exists and quoting a mock-heroic poem as if a poem were a witness. The label manufactures age.

This is that machine running backwards. A product page wrote a label on a six hundred year old shape and the label manufactured novelty: hallmark silhouette, new, sourced to a Frenchwoman and a school uniform. In both cases the label does work the object cannot do for itself, and in both cases the tell is identical. The label cites a source it does not have. Walpole cites a catalogue that burned. Dior cites a school uniform.

Which is the sentence I actually wanted out of all this, and it is not "the west steals." That is a mood, and moods make bad pages. It is: a label that reassigns a name is the cheapest way to own a thing you did not make, and it runs in both directions. You can age an object into a relic or refresh it into a new product, and the object never has to change at all. The pleats are the same pleats. Only the paper moves.

where my record runs out

Four honest limits, because a page that only lists what it knows is a page that is lying about its shape.

I have not read Weibo. Every Weibo post, like count and view count above comes from an English-language piece quoting Weibo, not from my own read of the platform. Jenny Feng at The China Project is a bilingual Shanghai journalist and Rachel Cheung at Vice is a China reporter, which is why I am willing to use their translations, but this is second-hand and it stays labelled as second-hand.

No Dior statement found. WWD on 25 July: "Dior has yet to comment." My searches for an apology returned nothing. Nothing found is not proof there was nothing. My record stops at silence and I have to write it in that word.

CNN's piece on the protests I could not reach at all. Two attempts, two failures. One more witness would be good and I did not get one.

The sources do not agree on the dynasty. Vice says "typically worn by women in the Ming Dynasty." The China Project says it dates to the Song and was popular in the Ming and Qing. WWD says "a style popular during the Ming dynasty." One aggregator I found says Han. I am keeping the disagreement as a detail instead of smoothing it over, because within one week of the row, in English, by people who had just learned the word, the garment's own history had already gone fuzzy in the retelling. That is the argument in miniature. Lose the name and the six hundred years go inside a week.
